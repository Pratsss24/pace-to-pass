How quick each car was

The dose cannot be read off the laps two cars spend together, because those laps are the outcome: a car stuck behind another is slowed by being stuck behind it. So pace is measured apart and tested together — each car-race gets a line fitted to the laps it ran in clear air, and the advantage is the difference of two lines, each read at that car's own tyre age. The fitted field spans 2.57 s a lap at the median race, which is what a Formula 1 grid covers.

What being held up costs

The second bar is the control. Being close to another car costs nothing in itself — +0.049 s a lap for cars that were not quicker anyway. The cost appears only where the car behind had the pace to go faster and could not: +0.253 s a lap. Pooling the two, which is the obvious thing to do, dilutes it to almost nothing.

At the mean opening gap of 1.24 s, the fitted curve crosses even odds at +0.40 seconds a lap. The intercept is +0.013: two cars of identical pace running nose to tail are a coin toss, which is what it ought to be and was not imposed.

The dose is measured, not observed

Fitting each car's line twice — once on its odd clear laps, once on its even ones — gives two independent measurements of the same quantity. They correlate 0.877, leaving noise of 0.376 s a lap against signal of 1.001. Reliability is 0.876, so the fitted slope is pulled toward zero by about that factor: the true slope is nearer 2.46 and the threshold nearer +0.35 s a lap.

That column is not a ranking. Jointly, the 14 circuit terms give F 0.909, p 0.549. The fitted thresholds span 0.708 s a lap; shuffling the circuit labels across episodes — which destroys any real circuit effect and keeps everything else — gives a median span of 0.699, and beats the real spread 47% of the time. One season cannot resolve circuit differences, and the apparent ordering is what noise produces on its own.

What the guards were worth

A per-car line read outside the laps and tyre ages it was fitted on returned pace advantages of twenty-five seconds a lap. Two per cent of the rows were enough to flatten the central slope by a factor of five. Refusing implausible advantages recovers most of it; refusing extrapolation altogether recovers the rest, and the estimate then stops moving as the rule is tightened further.
